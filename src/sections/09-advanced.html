ARTICLE 08

Advanced additional training

Product management and design are no longer a separate track. They are advanced training offered on top of the Software Development Engineer curriculum.

Every Marine here trains as a Software Development Engineer. Product management and design are the skills that make an engineer useful on a balanced team — knowing who the user is, what problem is worth solving, and how to shape a solution someone can actually use. This training covers PM basics, design basics, visual and UI design principles, collaborative leadership, and the tooling that goes with them.

Product management basics

  • Know the user before the solution. Discovery comes first — generative research to learn what people actually do, not what they say they would do.
  • Frame the problem, not the feature. Jobs to be Done describes what a user is hiring the product to accomplish.
  • Story mapping. Lay the user's journey out horizontally and slice releases vertically, so every release delivers a whole thin experience rather than half a feature.
  • Roadmaps are direction, not dates. A roadmap communicates intent and sequence. It is a planning instrument, not a commitment schedule.
  • Backlog management. An ordered, groomed backlog is the team's working agreement about what comes next and why.
  • Balanced team. Engineer, product manager, and designer share the decision. None of the three outranks the problem.

Design basics

  • Design research. Generative research to find the problem; evaluative research — usability testing — to find out whether your answer works.
  • Information architecture. How content is structured, labeled, and navigated. Most “confusing UI” is an IA problem.
  • Interaction design. Cognition, perception, and memory drive behavior. Design the interaction around how people actually process what is in front of them.
  • Visual design. Typography, color theory, hierarchy, and spacing — the universal principles that make an interface readable before it is decorative.
  • UI design. Navigation, forms, data display, and feedback patterns; micro-interactions and UX writing.
  • Responsive design. One product, every screen size. Layout adapts; the content and its priority do not change.
  • Design systems. Style guides and pattern libraries so the product stays coherent as more hands touch it.

Design principles

HierarchyEvery screen has one most important thing. Size, weight, color, and position tell the user what it is before they read a word.
ConsistencyThe same action looks and behaves the same way everywhere. Users learn a product once, not once per screen.
ContrastDifference carries meaning — between text and background, between a primary and a secondary action. Insufficient contrast is both a design failure and an accessibility failure.
Proximity & alignmentThings that belong together sit together. Alignment creates the invisible structure that makes a layout feel deliberate.
FeedbackEvery action gets a response. The user should never wonder whether the system heard them.
Constraint & affordanceMake the right action obvious and the wrong action difficult. The interface should suggest its own use.
SimplicityRemove until removing breaks it. Complexity that the user has to absorb is complexity you failed to absorb for them.

Ethics & accessibility

You are building for Marines and for the public that Marines serve. A product that excludes part of that audience has failed, regardless of how well it performs.

  • Section 508. Federal law. Any electronic or information technology the government develops, procures, or uses must be accessible to people with disabilities. This is a requirement on your work, not a preference.
  • WCAG. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines are the measurable standard behind 508 — perceivable, operable, understandable, robust. Design and test against them from the start; retrofitting accessibility costs far more than building it in.
  • Design ethics. No dark patterns. Do not design an interface that misleads a user into an action they did not intend, and do not collect more than the mission requires.
  • Inclusive by default. Sufficient color contrast, keyboard navigation, screen-reader support, and plain language are baseline, not enhancements.
  • Balanced team collaboration. Ethical standards in UX and PM are a shared responsibility. Anyone on the team can and should raise a concern about the work.
